# The Night Shift at the Lost and Found

The central station's lost-and-found closes at midnight, which is a lie Clara
tells the sign. Someone has to be there when the last train empties its
pockets.

In eleven years she has catalogued 4,118 umbrellas, 962 gloves (never in
pairs), one wedding dress, and a prosthetic leg that was claimed in under
four minutes by a man who ran better than most people she knew.

The form has three fields: ITEM, DESCRIPTION, WHERE FOUND. On her first
winter, an old clerk named Yusuf taught her the fourth field, the one that
isn't printed.

"What people lost," he said, "is never the object."

Tonight, a Tuesday, the shelf brings her a paperback novel with a boarding
pass for a flight that left nine years ago tucked at page 214. A child's
shoe, left foot, size unknown to its owner. A phone at four percent, ringing
and ringing, which she is not allowed to answer.

At 3:40 AM a man in a good coat comes in for the eleventh time for the same
black umbrella. Clara has it in her hand already.

"You know," she says, "I could just give it to you to keep."

He looks at the umbrella like it's a dog that keeps running back. "Then I'd
have nothing to come back for."

She writes the fourth field on his ticket, the one that isn't printed:
*comes for the walk, not the umbrella.*

After he leaves she counts the shelf, which is her real job. Under ITEM, the
objects. Under the field that isn't printed: *the apology he never made. The
book she was too busy to finish. The flight they missed on purpose and lied
about after.*

People lose objects, Yusuf used to say. Objects keep the rest.

At dawn the first train sighs into the station and empties its pockets
again, and Clara opens the window, and takes down what they've left, and
writes it all down carefully, so that someday, someone can come back for it.

Most of them do.
