Event planning checklist: the order that actually works

Most event checklists fail, and not because they forget the napkins. They fail because they are lists of things instead of an order of operations. The cake ordered before the guest count is known, the venue booked before the budget ceiling exists. This is the checklist that respects dependencies - do it in this order and most emergencies never happen.

The three numbers that run every event

Before any checklist item, three numbers must exist, because every later decision hangs on them:

  • The guest count. A working estimate, not a wish. Every vendor quote, every seat, every plate is this number times money.
  • The per-head budget. Total budget divided by guests. When a quote arrives, you check it against this number, not against hope.
  • The table capacity. Seats per table times tables in the room. Seating plans break when this number is discovered late.
RSVP math that saves money: roughly 15-20% of yeses will not show. Plan catering and seating for the confirmed count, not the invited count - but keep a 10% buffer of seats, not meals. Seats are cheap; wasted plates are not.

The checklist, in dependency order

8+ weeks out: the frame

  • Set the date (and one backup date before falling in love with a venue).
  • Set the budget ceiling - a real number, written down, with a 10% contingency line already spent in your head.
  • Draft the guest list. It can change; it cannot not exist.

6 weeks out: the commitments

  • Book the venue against the guest estimate and table capacity, not the dream.
  • Send saves or invites with a reply-by date three weeks before the event.
  • Open the quote log: every vendor gets a row the day they quote.

4 weeks out: the counts

  • Lock catering against confirmed counts. Get the cancellation cutoff date in writing.
  • Confirm rentals (tables, chairs, linens) against the seating plan, not the floor plan pdf.
  • First seating draft: guests to tables, seats checked against capacity.

2 weeks out: the reconciliation

  • Final guest count to every vendor - one email, all numbers, written.
  • Budget pass: every quote logged against its plan line. Anything over plan gets a decision now, not on the day.
  • Seating final: seats equal capacity. Every table balanced for the guests' sake, not the spreadsheet's.

Week of: the run sheet

  • One page: who arrives, when, who calls whom, what happens if it rains.
  • Vendor confirmations re-sent - a confirmation read is not a confirmation received.
  • Payments staged: what is paid before, what is due on the day, in envelopes or scheduled.

The day after: the close-out

  • Reconcile the budget: quotes, actuals, deposits returned.
  • Thank-you notes while names are still warm.
  • Write down the three things that went wrong. That list is the next event's head start.

Why a checklist is not a workbook

A checklist tells you what to do. An event still needs a living document - one guest list everyone updates, seating that checks itself against table capacity, a budget that shows quotes versus plan as they arrive. The checklist is the order; the workbook is the machine that runs it.
