The freelance proposal that gets signed

Most proposals read like price lists with a personality. The ones that get signed have a fixed skeleton, and the skeleton has an order. Change the order and you start negotiations one step behind.

The seven parts, in order

  • 1. Their words. The problem restated in the client's own language, first line. They should recognize themselves before they see a number.
  • 2. The outcome. One sentence on which lever moves - revenue, cost, or risk - and roughly how far.
  • 3. The shape of the work. Phases or deliverables, short and noun-heavy. No day-by-day theater.
  • 4. One number. A total for the default scope. No line-item hourly maths - line items invite line-item bargaining.
  • 5. What's not included. Two or three honest exclusions. This line is where scope creep dies.
  • 6. The terms. Payment schedule, the two revision rounds included, response times.
  • 7. The expiry. Prices hold for 14 days - not pressure, just the fact that your calendar is finite.

Say it like weather

"This comes to $4,800 - half to start, half on delivery, and the first draft lands two weeks after the deposit." Then stop talking. The pause after a price is the sound of a decision being made. The person who fills it has just offered to negotiate against themselves.

When they push back

"It's more than we expected" is almost always a scope mismatch, not a price objection: ask which part of the scope they expected it to cover, then cut scope, never margin. "Another quote is half yours" deserves a blessing, not a discount: "If the cheaper quote includes that, take it." A number that folds on the first push was never a price - it was a wish with a decimal point.
