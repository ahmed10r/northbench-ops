The one-page weekly review for a one-person business

Weekly reviews fail by ambition: thirty-metric dashboards die by week three. The version that survives asks five questions and fits on one page - and the same five questions, every week, is where the pattern-recognition comes from.

The short answer

  • Same time every week, or it doesn't happen. Friday afternoon or Monday morning - pick the one your calendar defends.
  • Five questions, one page: what came in, what's owed, what's next, what's at risk, what gets better.
  • Money in and money owed come from the ledgers, not from memory. If the ledger is thin, that's the finding.
  • 'What's at risk' gets one honest sentence per risk. A risk written down is a plan; a risk felt is anxiety.
  • The review ends with the first action for Monday, chosen now, while the week's context is still loaded.

The page, question by question

  1. Money in: what landed this week, from whom, against what invoice. Total it. Compare to last week only - trends need weeks, not days.
  2. Money owed: every invoice outstanding, its age, and its next action. Anything over 30 days gets a chase this week, no exceptions - that rule alone pays for the review.
  3. Work next: the projects active this coming week and the one deliverable that matters most in each. Two per project maximum; a list of ten is a list of zero.
  4. At risk: the one to three things that could go wrong - a client gone quiet, a deadline dependent on someone else, a pipeline with nothing in it. One sentence each, one action each.
  5. What gets better: the single process that annoyed you most this week, and the smallest fix. One improvement a week is fifty a year; that compounds.

Why one page is the point

  • A one-page review gets done on the bad weeks - and the bad weeks are when it matters most.
  • The same five questions produce comparable pages, so week 12 can be compared to week 2. Patterns only show in repetition.
  • Writing by hand or in one plain file both work. The dashboard is not the product; the noticing is.

Questions people actually ask

Twenty minutes really enough?

For the review itself, yes - if the ledgers are current. Twenty minutes of review plus ten minutes of ledger catch-up is the honest budget, and it's still less than one lost invoice costs.

What about tracking marketing and pipeline?

Pipeline is 'what's at risk' when it's thin - you'll notice. Give marketing its own simple counter (posts out, leads in) rather than swelling the weekly page; the review is for steering, not for everything.
