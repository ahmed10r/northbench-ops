Which of these files do you need first, and which can wait

A shelf of files is not a system. Two files you keep current will do more for you than ten you open once - so choose by the job that costs you most this month.

The short answer

  • Pick the job that goes wrong most often, not the one that sounds most impressive.
  • Start with money or with time. Those two produce the reason to keep going.
  • Two files maximum for the first month.
  • Add a third only when the first two are boring, which means you update them without thinking about it.
  • Every extra file needs a moment in your week, or it dies quietly in a downloads folder.

Match the job to the file

  • Chasing unpaid invoices, and knowing what a job really earned: the freelancer tracker.
  • Knowing where the month went and what you owe: the household workbook.
  • Posting consistently without last-minute panic: the content calendar.
  • Getting replies from strangers about work: the outreach system.
  • Applying for jobs without losing track of who owes you an answer: the job tracker.
  • Selling things online and being found for them: the listing file.
  • A habit you keep without a phone app: the printable planner.
  • Turning what you know into a file someone pays for: the launch kit.

The order that works

If you cannot decide, put money first. Money facts are the ones that change decisions: whether a client is worth keeping, whether a price is too low, whether a month was as good as it felt while it was happening. Time comes second, because a week organised badly is recoverable and a year of underpricing is harder to undo.

Then leave the rest. A file you add in month three should be one that makes the first two easier to keep, not a third thing to maintain.

  • Month one: the one file that answers the question you ask yourself most often.
  • Month two: the file that makes the first one easier to keep, usually a calendar or a tracker.
  • Month three: read both for twenty minutes and delete what you have not touched.

The bundle question, honestly

Bundles exist because they cost less per file. They only help if you would genuinely use several of them in the same season, which is rarer than the bundle page suggests. The test takes ten seconds: name the three files you would open in the next thirty days. If you cannot name three, the bundle is a shelf ornament for now.

If you are pricing a bundle of your own, the free calculator shows how a bundle price compares with the parts, so you can see whether you are rewarding a buyer or quietly undercutting yourself.

  • Ask before buying a bundle, not after.
  • Buying more files does not create the habit of using them.
  • If you already own two parts, count the difference rather than the bundle price.
