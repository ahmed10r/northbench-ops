How to work out your net worth on one page, without the jargon

Net worth is subtraction with better manners. One page, four columns, ten minutes a quarter - and the figure becomes useful instead of dramatic.

The short answer

  • Add up everything you could turn into money this month if you had to. Add up everything you owe. Subtract the second from the first.
  • Count what you own outright, not what you paid for it.
  • Count the full balance you owe, not this month's instalment.
  • Recheck it once a quarter, on the same day, in the same file.
  • The direction matters more than the figure today.

The four columns

  1. Cash: current accounts, savings, and money sitting in payment apps, each with the date you checked it.
  2. Sellable things: anything you could turn into money within a week, valued at what it would actually fetch rather than what it cost.
  3. Owed to you: unpaid invoices and money you have lent, with an honest note about whether it will arrive.
  4. Owed by you: card balances, loans, instalments, and any tax you already know is coming.
  5. One line at the bottom: total minus total. That is the number, and it is arithmetic, not a judgement about you.

Why a page beats an app for this

The figure is meant to inform a decision every three months, so an account you must log into daily is the wrong shape for it. A dashboard that updates constantly invites you to watch it, and watching a number that moves with the markets teaches you nothing you can act on.

A page in the file you already keep has one advantage that no app has: it is boring. Boring is why it survives a bad month.

  • Write the date beside every value. A stale figure is worse than no figure, because you will trust it.
  • Anything behind a login gets checked in a good month and ignored in a bad one.
  • Keep one line of history per quarter. Four lines a year are enough to see a shape.

What to do with the number

Two useful things, and no more. First, it shows whether what you owe is shrinking faster than what you hold grows. That comparison is arithmetic, not advice, and it is usually enough to change one decision.

  • Do not set a target you cannot state in one sentence.
  • Do not include a house or car you would never sell. Counting things you intend to keep makes the figure flatter you and move less.

Questions people actually ask

Should I include my home or my car?

Only if you would genuinely sell it. Most people would not, and including it hides the change you are trying to see. If you are unsure, leave it out and note the choice in the file.

How often should I redo it?

Quarterly, on a fixed date. Monthly turns into noise and a chore, and yearly is too slow to change anything you do.
