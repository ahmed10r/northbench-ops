How to stop scope creep with one paragraph

Scope creep is not a client being evil. It is a client discovering what they want, one small request at a time, in a relationship with no rule for pricing discovery. The rule is one paragraph.

The paragraph

Put this in the proposal, near the terms, and say it out loud once on the kickoff call:

  • 'Everything in this project is listed in the scope above. If something comes up that is not on the list, I will quote it separately, in writing, before doing it - and you decide whether it is worth it. That way the price stays the price.'
  • That is the whole magic. It is not a no. It is a priced yes, decided by the person who is paying.

Why it works

  • It removes the awkwardness from both sides: they do not have to wonder if they are asking for too much, and you do not have to wonder how to say no.
  • It converts the worst kind of work - free, unthanked, unrecorded - into a decision with a number attached.
  • Good clients respect it, because professionals have terms and amateurs have moods.
  • It survives in writing. Memory does not, and memory is where scope creep does its real damage: both sides remembering a different conversation.

What creep actually costs, in arithmetic

  • Two extra hours a week, at your hourly rate, over a two-month project, is a meaningful slice of the project price - given away in pieces too small to argue about.
  • The requests are always small. That is the mechanism: nobody asks for a free second project; they ask for a free extra page, eleven times.
  • Track the asks for one project without saying no to any of them, just writing them down. The total is usually the best argument you will ever have for the paragraph.

How to say it without losing anyone

  1. Introduce the rule before any request exists - in the proposal and on the kickoff call, when it is clearly about professionalism and not about them.
  2. When the first request comes, thank it, then price it the same day in two lines. Speed is what makes it feel like service instead of resistance.
  3. Quote small changes cheaply but never free; the price can be twenty dollars, and the number existing matters more than its size.
  4. After the project, note in your records how many change orders happened. That number is how you price the next project for the same client.

Questions people actually ask

What if the client pushes back on the paragraph itself?

Ask, plainly, which part they disagree with: that changes get quoted, or that they decide. It is almost always the fear of being nickel-and-dimed - so offer one small included change per phase as goodwill, written down, and keep the rule.

Does this apply to fixed-price work too?

Especially to fixed-price work. Hourly projects meter creep automatically, because extra hours show up on the invoice. Fixed price with no change rule is an open door with a welcome mat.
